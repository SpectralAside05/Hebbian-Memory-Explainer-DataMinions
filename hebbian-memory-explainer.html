When wiring becomes memory

How a network can remember several recent things without writing anything to a notebook, a cache, or a trained parameter - just by temporarily changing how strongly its own neurons are connected.

The one-sentence claim this page teaches: A network whose connection strengths update online, after every input, using a local Hebbian rule (Δw ∝ activity × activity) can store several recent patterns directly in its weights and later reconstruct one from a partial cue - but its capacity is limited, so storing too many patterns makes them interfere and corrupts recall of all of them. Making the stored activity sparse and non-negative - the choice Dragon Hatchling (BDH) makes - measurably reduces that interference.

Where this actually shows up in BDH

System: Dragon Hatchling (BDH), Pathway, Sept. 2025 (arXiv:2509.26507) - and its later reasoning variant BDH-CQ (arXiv:2608.09888).

BDH does not carry a growing key-value cache the way a standard Transformer does. Its working memory is the state of the synapse graph itself: attention is reformulated as a Hebbian update to synaptic strength, so recent co-activity between "neuron particles" literally strengthens the connection between them, and that connection is what gets read back later in the same context. This is exactly the mechanism this sandbox simplifies to a 35-unit toy: Δw ∝ activity(i) × activity(j), applied online, with no gradient step and no backward pass.

The two knobs in this sandbox map onto two things the BDH paper reports explicitly:

  • Sparse, non-negative activity. The paper reports that BDH's activation vectors are sparse and positive, with roughly 5% of neurons active at a time, and that activity level varies with how predictable the input is, rather than following a fixed budget. This sandbox's "sparse retrieval" toggle is a deliberately simplified, non-official stand-in for that constraint - a k-winners-take-all nonlinearity, not BDH's actual activation function - used only to show why a sparsity constraint of this general kind reduces crosstalk between overlapping memories.
  • Monosemantic synapses. The paper reports that individual synapses in trained BDH models become selective for recognisable concepts rather than firing for many unrelated inputs - the graph-level analogue of "this connection means one thing." That's the same reason sparse writes in the sandbox interfere less: fewer, more selective active units share fewer of their connections across different stored patterns.

BDH-CQ's contextual memory (used for in-context learning on ARC-AGI-style tasks) is reported as an evolving recurrent state built from exactly this family of update: each demonstration updates a fixed-size memory additively, related in the paper's own framing to fast-weight and linear-attention views of contextual association, and no parameters are changed at inference - adaptation lives in that recurrent state, not in the weights that were trained. The sandbox's "store, then decay" behaviour is a toy version of that same idea: fast, temporary, capacity-limited state versus slow, trained, durable weights.

Honesty check: this sandbox is an independent 35-unit educational toy, built for this explainer, not a reproduction of BDH and not run on any BDH checkpoint. The Hebbian outer-product rule, the sparsity-reduces-interference effect, and the capacity trade-off are real and well established (Hopfield-style associative memory; Schlag, Irie & Schmidhuber's fast-weight-programmer equivalence). What's illustrative rather than official is the specific k-winners-take-all mechanism used to stand in for BDH's own activation function - the real BDH nonlinearity and exact sparsity dynamics are defined in the primary paper, not reproduced here.

Limitations of the sandbox itself

  • 35 units is tiny. Real associative-memory capacity scales with network size in ways this demo can only gesture at.
  • Synchronous settling can oscillate for some corrupted cues; the demo caps iterations and reports whichever state it lands on, which is itself a real property of this kind of recurrent recall, not a bug being hidden from you.
  • "Sparse mode" here is a retrieval-time nonlinearity chosen for teaching clarity. It is not BDH's activation function, and the exact numbers (~5% active, degree distribution, etc.) come only from the primary paper, not from this toy.
